Prophet Yaqub (Jacob), son of Isaac and grandson of Abraham, is a central figure whose life bridges the early patriarchs to the Twelve Tribes of Israel. His story begins with a deep-seated conflict with his twin brother, Esau. The reasons for this animosity are presented with different emphases in Islamic traditions and the accounts of the People of the Book, revealing distinct theological framings of his destiny and character.

Fleeing from his brother's wrath, Jacob experienced a profound vision that would affirm his divine calling. Alone and vulnerable, he dreamt of a ladder reaching to the heavens, with angels ascending and descending. In this vision, Allah directly promised him the land and a blessed, numerous lineage. This was not just a dream, but a divine covenant that shaped the rest of his life.

"Upon awakening, he made a solemn vow: if he returned home safely, he would build a temple for Allah on that very spot and dedicate a tenth of all his possessions to Him. He anointed the stone he had slept on and named the place Bethel, the 'House of Allah'."

During his twenty years in Haran serving his uncle Laban, Jacob's family grew to form the progenitors of the twelve tribes of Israel. Through his two wives, Leah and Rachel, and their respective handmaidens, Zilpah and Bilha, twelve sons and one daughter were born, fulfilling the divine promise of a great nation. Jacob's journey was one of immense trials that forged his character and faith. From enduring years of deception by his uncle Laban to a physical and spiritual struggle with an angel, these events transformed him from a fugitive into Israel, the patriarch of a nation.

Jacob worked for seven years to marry Rachel, the love of his life, only to be deceived by his uncle Laban into marrying her elder sister, Leah. He then worked another seven years for Rachel. Despite Laban's trickery, Jacob's family and wealth grew, a testament to his patience and divine favor.

When Jacob decided to leave, Laban attempted to cheat him of his wages. Yet, through what the People of the Book describe as a miracle, Jacob prospered. He used peeled rods at watering troughs to influence the offspring of the flocks, resulting in a vast number of animals with the desired markings, fulfilling his agreed-upon payment.

On the eve of his reunion with Esau, Jacob had a mysterious and intense encounter, wrestling with a man until dawn, who was revealed to be an angel. Though left with a permanent limp, he emerged from the struggle with a new name and a renewed identity.

The angel declared, "After today you shall not be called anything but Israel." This name change signified his new status as one who has striven with the divine and prevailed, marking him as the head of a covenanted people.

Fearing Esau's approach with 400 men, Jacob prayed earnestly and prepared a lavish gift. His humility and supplication were answered. When the brothers met, Esau ran to Jacob, embraced him, and they wept. The long-standing rift was healed through forgiveness and humility.

The Quran provides the definitive theological understanding of Prophet Yaqub's faith. It consistently portrays him not as a Jew or Christian—labels that did not exist in his time—but as a "Muslim Hanifan," one who submits to the One True God, continuing the pure monotheistic tradition of his grandfather, Abraham.

Chapter 2 (Al-Baqarah) highlights Jacob's dying injunction to his sons, confirming their commitment to the same One God worshipped by their forefathers. It directly challenges the notion that the patriarchs were Jews or Christians, asserting they were "Muslims" (those who submit).

"And this was the legacy from Abraham to his sons, and so was it from Jacob; 'O my sons! Allah has chosen for you the true religion, then die not except in the Faith of Islam (as Muslims -- Islamic Monotheism).'" (Quran 2:132)

Chapter 3 (Ali 'Imran) makes a logical argument: How could Abraham be a Jew or a Christian when the Torah and the Gospel were revealed long after his time? It defines his true religion as "Hanifan" (pure Islamic Monotheism).

"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a true Muslim Hanifan (Islamic Monotheism), to worship none but Allah Alone and he was not of the polytheists." (Quran 3:67)

Chapter 16 (An-Nahl) describes Abraham as an "Ummah" (a leader embodying righteous qualities or a nation), "obedient to Allah, Hanifan (to worship none but Allah), and he was not one of those who were Al Mushrikeen (polytheists)".

"Then, We have inspired you (O Muhammad ﷺ saying): 'Follow the religion of Abraham Hanifan (Islamic Monotheism to worship none but Allah) and he was not of the Mushrikeen (polytheists).'" (Quran 16:123)

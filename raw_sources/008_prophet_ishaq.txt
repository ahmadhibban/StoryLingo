Prophet Ishaq (Isaac) is a revered patriarch in the Abrahamic faiths, serving as a critical link in the prophetic chain. While foundational scriptures provide a framework for his life, much of the detailed narrative comes from later interpretive traditions. This exploration delves into two such significant sources: the accounts of "Quranic Commentators" and the traditions of "the People of the Book." By comparing these narratives, we can gain a richer, more nuanced understanding of how his story is told, the theological points each tradition emphasizes, and the profound shared history that connects them. The marriage of Prophet Ishaq to Rebekah was a pivotal event, ensuring the continuation of the patriarchal lineage. From the union of Ishaq and Rebekah came twin sons, Esau and Jacob. Both traditions agree on their names, but the People of the Book's account provides additional genealogical details that are foundational to the identity of future nations. This section highlights how each tradition introduces these two pivotal figures.

This account confirms the birth of the twins, named Esau (Al-Eis) and Jacob (Yaqub). The focus of this narrative tradition quickly moves to the relationship between them, rather than their specific lineage or the etymology of their names.

Esau's Lineage: He is identified as the "father of Rum," providing a genealogical origin for another major people.

Jacob's Identity: It is explicitly stated that Jacob's name means "Israel," directly linking him to the "people of Israel."

Theological Emphasis: These details are crucial for establishing national and religious identity, connecting the patriarchs directly to the peoples and nations that would follow.

The relationship between Esau and Jacob was fraught with tension that ultimately led to their separation. The Quranic Commentators provide a specific theological reason for this conflict, elevating it from a simple sibling rivalry to a matter of divine favor and destiny. This distinction is one of the most significant divergences in the narratives.

Root of Animosity: Esau "disliked the fact that Jacob was favoured by his father and by Allah with prophethood."

Escalation: The ill-feeling grew so severe that Esau threatened to kill his brother.

Consequence: Fearing for his life, Jacob fled the country. This frames the conflict as being centered on spiritual inheritance.

The account mentions that "ill feelings developed between the two brothers" after they grew into manhood, but the provided source material does not elaborate on the specific cause with the same theological depth as the Quranic Commentators. The focus remains on the fact of their rivalry rather than the divine reasoning behind it.
